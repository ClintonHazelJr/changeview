# A field guide to change management systems, and why most of them weren't built with you in mind

Search "change management software" and watch the results turn into a pile-up. Some are
enterprise platforms built for portfolio dashboards. Some are IT tools where "change"
means a server configuration, not a person. Somewhere in that mess, you're just trying to
find something that understands what you actually do all day.

## Two things wearing the same name

The first imposter is the digital adoption platform, tools that lay guided tooltips over
another piece of software so someone learns it faster. Genuinely useful, worth having.
Not built for the part of your job that happens before anyone opens that software:
scoping who's affected, mapping stakeholders, drafting the comms that make the rollout
land at all.

The second imposter is IT change management, ServiceNow and its relatives. In that
world, "change" means a technical request, approved by a change advisory board, to modify
a system. If you land here searching for a tool for your actual work, you're standing in
the wrong building, even though the sign out front uses your exact words.

## The thing that's actually missing

What you're really looking for, a system built around the real shape of your work,
impact assessment, stakeholder management, comms planning, adoption tracking, barely
exists as a mature category yet. The methodology got fifty years to mature. The software
never got the same runway.

What you'll find instead sits at two extremes. On one end, enterprise platforms with real
depth and enterprise pricing to match, built assuming a dedicated team running a handful
of massive transformations a year. On the other, a generic project management tool with
some custom fields bolted on, something that can hold a task list but doesn't know the
difference between a stakeholder and an assignee, or between someone finishing a training
module and someone actually adopting the change.

## What to actually check for

Skip the feature-count comparison. Ask instead: does it understand impact at the level of
departments and people, not just projects and tasks? Does a comms plan live somewhere
separate from a generic to-do list? Does it treat adoption as its own real thing,
distinct from "did they click complete"? And, quietly the most important question, is it
priced for how you actually work, one client or one department at a time, not one
enterprise contract at a time?

You didn't choose this profession because the tooling was elegant. Most days, it's the
opposite. That gap is closing, slowly, mostly because people who've lived the problem
firsthand got tired of waiting and built the thing themselves.
