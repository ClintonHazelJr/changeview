# AI didn't create a new kind of change. It just made everything move faster.

Picture the last "quick" software rollout you ran. A copilot bolted onto the CRM, an
assistant dropped into the ticketing system, a chatbot parked on the intranet. Somewhere
in the kickoff meeting, someone said the words "this is really just an IT project," and
you felt your stomach sink a little, because you already knew how this was going to go.

You'd be right to worry. And you'd also be the only person in the room who understood
why.

## The tool was never the hard part

Here's the thing nobody in that meeting wants to hear: deploying the software is the easy
80%. Flip a switch, provision the licenses, send the "we're live" email. What's actually
hard, what's always been hard, is that AI tools ask people to change how they think about
their own competence, not just which button they click. A support agent using an AI
assistant isn't learning a new field in a form. They're being asked to trust a
recommendation they didn't generate themselves, and then to feel confident enough to
override it when it's wrong. That's not a training problem. That's an identity problem,
the quiet fear of "does this mean I'm being replaced," sitting underneath every "any
questions?" in the room.

You already have fifty years of frameworks for exactly this kind of fear. ADKAR, Kotter,
Prosci, none of it wasted, all of it still true. What's changed isn't the psychology.
It's the clock. A CRM migration used to hand you six months to build comms and training
around that fear. An AI rollout hands you six weeks, because the business case was
"everyone else is already doing this" and nobody budgeted for the part where people need
to feel safe first.

## What doesn't change, even now

You still need to know who's actually affected, and how badly. You still need comms that
speak to one team's specific fear, not a company-wide email that speaks to no one's. You
still need to measure whether people actually trust the tool, not just whether they sat
through the training. None of that got easier. It just got less optional, faster.

## Where it actually gets easier

Here's the one genuine gift in all of this: the tools you use to do your own job got
better too. Drafting a comms plan for five different audiences used to eat an afternoon.
Now the first draft can take minutes, not because the thinking got any less important,
but because the blank page stopped being where you lost half your day.

Don't mistake that for permission to skip the thinking. Spend the time you got back
talking to the people who are actually going to be affected, the ones with the quiet
worry in the kickoff meeting. Find out what's really going to make them resist, three
weeks before it's too late to do anything about it, not three weeks after.

AI rollouts are coming to your desk whether you're ready or not. Treat it like an IT
ticket and you'll have an adoption problem by the next quarterly review. Treat it like
the change it actually is, the same way you always have, just faster now, and you'll be
the person in the room who saw it coming.
