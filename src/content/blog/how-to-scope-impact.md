# How to actually scope the impact of a change, before it scopes you

I've watched more change plans go sideways in the first two weeks than I can count, and
it's almost never the training or the comms that were the problem. It's that nobody
scoped the impact properly before the plan got written. Here's how I actually do it.

## Start with departments, not tasks

Everyone wants to jump straight to a project plan, tasks, owners, dates. Don't. The first
question is simpler: which departments does this change actually touch, and how hard does
it hit each one? A system migration might flatten Operations and barely graze Finance,
who just need a new login. Treat both the same in your plan and you'll under-resource the
group that actually needed you most.

## Rate severity honestly, not urgently

Don't stop at one overall impact score per department. Break it down: does this change
the org structure itself? Someone's actual role? Their process? The systems they touch?
Their physical environment? A department can be hit hard on process and barely touched on
everything else, and that distinction changes what you plan for. High process impact,
low system impact means workflow retraining. Flip it, and you need a system walkthrough,
not a process overhaul.

I'll say the quiet part: it's tempting to mark everything "high" so the change program
looks like it matters. Resist it. A plan where everything is high severity is a plan
where nothing is actually prioritized, and everyone can tell.

## Write the current state and future state down, literally

For every impacted department, write two sentences: what does their day actually look
like right now, in their own words if you can get them, and what will it look like after.
This sounds obvious. It's the step I see skipped most often, and it's the difference
between comms that actually land and comms nobody remembers. "You'll be trained on the
new system" says nothing. "You'll stop chasing the shared spreadsheet and log requests
straight into the portal, cutting your average turnaround from two days to same-day" is
the kind of line people actually repeat to their manager.

## Let the impact drive the plan, don't invent the plan separately

Once you know who's impacted, how badly, and in exactly what way, the rest should fall
out of that, not get dreamed up on its own. High people-impact teams need more sessions,
smaller groups. High process-impact teams need documentation before they need a demo.
Your comms should speak to what each group actually cares about, not one company-wide
email trying to be everything to everyone and landing with no one.

## Why it's worth the slow start

Scoping feels like the part everyone wants to rush past to get to "real" execution. It's
the part that decides whether execution works at all. Skip it and you're planning
training off guesses. Do it right and every decision after gets easier, because you
already wrote down who needs what. You're not guessing anymore.
