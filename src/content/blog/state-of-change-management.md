# The state of change management: rising demand, flat headcount, unchanged tooling

Three trends are converging on the change management profession right now, and they
don't fit together comfortably. Demand for the discipline is at an all-time high.
Staffing behind it hasn't kept pace. And the software most practitioners rely on to do
the actual work has barely shifted in a decade.

## Demand: no longer in question

Organizations have largely stopped debating whether change management is necessary.
Digital transformation, AI adoption, restructuring, and M&A activity keep a steady
pipeline of work requiring managed change moving through the market. The open question
has shifted from "do we need this" to "who's actually going to deliver it."

That second question is where the strain shows. A decade ago, a large transformation
typically justified a dedicated change team led by a certified practitioner with real
budget behind them. Today, the same scope of work is increasingly assigned to a single
internal generalist, or outsourced to an independent consultant on a fixed fee, expected
to produce comparable outcomes with a fraction of the resourcing.

## The tooling gap, stated plainly

Despite the discipline's maturity, most of the actual delivery work still happens in
spreadsheets, slide decks, and email threads. This isn't a knowledge gap among
practitioners. It's a structural one: the software category was built and priced around
an older model, one dedicated team running one or two large transformations annually.
That model doesn't describe most of the market anymore.

The result is a profession running mature methodology, ADKAR, Kotter, well-documented
best practice, on infrastructure that hasn't meaningfully advanced since roughly 2010.

## Budget scrutiny is reshaping expectations

Change budgets are increasingly absorbed into broader project budgets rather than
standing independently, which subjects them to the same scrutiny as any other line item.
Practitioners are now expected to produce genuine adoption data rather than qualitative
assurance that a rollout "went well." Anecdote no longer clears a budget review on its
own.

## What actually differentiates practitioners right now

The differentiator in this environment isn't certification count, it's capacity: the
ability to run more concurrent change work, across more clients or departments, without
overhead scaling linearly alongside it. That requires better systems, not longer hours.
The methodology hasn't gotten any easier to execute. The expectation to execute it leaner
has increased substantially, and the tooling available to meet that expectation is only
now beginning to catch up.
