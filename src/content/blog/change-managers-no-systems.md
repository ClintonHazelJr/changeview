# Every department got its own system. Except the one whose whole job is managing everyone else's.

Sales got a CRM. Support got a ticketing system. Engineering got issue tracking. HR got
an HRIS. Every function that touches an organization at real scale eventually got
handed a system built specifically for how that function actually works.

Change management never did. And it's the one function whose entire job is helping every
other department survive disruption.

## Sit with that for a second

Your job is to help a sales team trust a new CRM, help support agents feel safe with a
new AI assistant, help finance adapt to a process that changes how they've worked for
years. You are, professionally, the person organizations hire to manage disruption for
everyone else. And if someone asked you right now what system you run your own work in,
what would you actually say? A shared spreadsheet? A slide deck template you've rebuilt
four times this year? Whatever project management tool happened to already be sitting in
the building when you arrived?

## It's not really your fault

Part of this is the tooling gap itself, the category simply never matured the way
adjacent ones did. But part of it is structural, and it's worth naming: you're often
brought in as the connective tissue between departments, not given one of your own to run
from. A team that owns a budget line gets to make the case for tooling spend. A function
that exists to serve every other function often has no natural home to make that case
from at all.

If you're independent, it's worse. Running change for three different clients means
there's no internal department behind you fighting for a line item, you're paying for
software, if you pay for it at all, straight out of your own margin. Most people in your
position don't. They just rebuild the spreadsheet for the fourth time this year and call
it Tuesday.

## What it's actually costing you

This isn't a minor inconvenience, even though it gets treated like one. Every spreadsheet
you rebuild from scratch is an hour you didn't spend talking to a stakeholder who needed
you. Every comms plan scattered across three email threads is a handoff waiting to fall
through the cracks. And every time someone asks how the rollout is going and your honest
answer is "let me check my notes," that's a small hit to your credibility, landing at
exactly the moment you're being asked to prove your value with data instead of a good
story.

## It doesn't need to be complicated

You don't need something heavier. Most of the enterprise options are already too much for
how you actually work day to day. What's missing is something built at your actual scale,
for one practitioner or a small team, that finally understands the real shape of the
work, impact, stakeholders, comms, adoption, without an enterprise contract or a
six-week implementation standing between you and using it.

Every other function got this years ago. You're overdue. You know that better than
anyone.
