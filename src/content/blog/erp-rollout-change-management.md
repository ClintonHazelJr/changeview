# You're implementing a new ERP. Here's the part nobody budgeted for.

Somewhere in the process of comparing ERP platforms, cloud migration timelines, and
integration costs, a question usually gets skipped: who's actually going to help two
hundred employees stop doing their jobs the old way? By the time anyone asks it, the
software's often already been chosen, and the change budget, if there is one, is an
afterthought bolted onto the technical rollout plan.

## The system is the easy part

Most ERP and business-system implementations are evaluated almost entirely on technical
criteria: features, integrations, cost, vendor support. All reasonable things to compare.
None of them predict whether the rollout actually succeeds, because the thing that
usually breaks an ERP implementation isn't the software. It's that the people using it
every day never really adopted the new way of working underneath it.

This isn't a niche problem. It's close to the default outcome when change management
gets treated as a footnote to a technical project instead of a parallel workstream with
its own plan.

## What actually needs to happen alongside the technical rollout

- **Impact scoping, department by department.** A new ERP rarely hits every team the same way. Finance might face a completely new close process. Warehouse staff might just need a new login. Treating both the same wastes resources on the group that didn't need much and under-serves the one that needed a lot.
- **Real communications, not one company-wide email.** Different departments need different messages, aimed at what's actually changing for them specifically, not a generic "we're upgrading our systems" announcement that speaks to no one in particular.
- **Training tied to the actual new process, not just the software interface.** Knowing where the buttons are in the new system isn't the same as knowing the new approval workflow that now runs through it.
- **Adoption tracking after go-live**, not just a go-live celebration and a hope that it sticks. The real test of an ERP rollout isn't the launch date, it's whether people are still using the old workaround three months later.

## Why this gets skipped

Change management often gets treated as optional because it's not a line item the
software vendor bills for, so it's easy to leave out of the budget entirely. The cost
doesn't disappear when that happens, it just shows up later, as low adoption, workarounds
that undermine the new system's value, and a technically successful implementation that
never actually changed how anyone works.

If you're planning a system rollout right now, the question worth asking isn't whether
you need change management. It's whether you're planning to pay for it upfront, or pay
for it later in the form of a rollout that technically launched and never actually took
hold.
